Final Philosophy of Instructional Technology

Learning, human judgment, and context come before technology.

My philosophy developed through more than ten years of teaching mathematics and physics in Nigerian secondary schools, studying through distance education, and designing technology-supported instruction.

People learn best when they connect new information with prior knowledge, understand concepts behind procedures, participate actively, learn with and from others, receive timely feedback, and gradually assume responsibility for learning. Technology supports this process when it removes a barrier, represents an abstract idea, supports practice or collaboration, makes thinking visible, or provides an accessible way to demonstrate understanding.

Meaning and Purpose of Instructional Technology

Educational technology involves the study and ethical practice of facilitating learning and improving performance through appropriate processes and resources (Januszewski & Molenda, 2008). This definition moves attention beyond devices toward analyzing problems, designing experiences, selecting resources, assessing learning, and revising instruction.

The same tool may support learning in one school and create a barrier in another. Zoom appeared useful for reaching students outside school, but unreliable electricity and poor internet excluded many intended learners. Kahoot!, in contrast, supported more formative questions and immediate feedback within limited lesson time. I therefore begin with the learning problem and conditions of use, not with the tool.

Learning and the Roles of Learners and Teachers

My view draws mainly from cognitivist, constructivist, and sociocultural perspectives while retaining selected behaviorist strategies. Learning requires attention, organization, retrieval, and connections with prior knowledge. Learners should investigate patterns, solve problems, explain their reasoning, and learn through interaction. Learning theories are not competitors from which a teacher must select only one; different learners, objectives, and tasks require different combinations of strategies (Ertmer & Newby, 2013).

Learners should ask questions, practice purposefully, collaborate, reflect on errors, explain their thinking, and gradually develop independence. The teacher plans, explains, demonstrates, questions, guides, motivates, assesses, and provides feedback. Technology may shift the teacher from the main information source toward designer and facilitator, but it does not replace professional judgment.

Teachers must recognize when productive struggle is supporting learning and when it is becoming frustration. I would intervene when a learner cannot explain the first step, repeatedly applies the same misconception after questioning, or begins to disengage from the task. I would first ask the learner to explain the first step, provide one targeted hint if the misconception persists, and remove that hint during the next problem to determine whether the learner can proceed independently. I would also structure collaboration so that confident students support their peers without dominating the learning process.

Engagement matters because students are more willing to enter and remain in an enjoyable, relevant environment. Enjoyment, however, does not prove understanding. Decorative features, excessive competition, or entertaining multimedia may distract from the concept. Words and visuals must be selected and organized within the limits of human information processing (Mayer, 2002).

Instructional Strategies and Evidence of Learning

My preferred strategies move learners from conceptual understanding to supported application and independence. I activate prior knowledge and use a problem, demonstration, or representation to make the concept meaningful. Questioning, modeling, guided practice, peer interaction, feedback, independent practice, and reflection follow according to the task. Repetition and correction can strengthen fluency, but practice should reinforce understanding rather than rote performance.

My physics teaching illustrates how the strategy should determine the tool. To investigate how impurities affect boiling point, students heated water, recorded its boiling point, added measured quantities of salt, and discussed the resulting change. A hands-on experiment provided the most direct evidence. For Boyle's law, a simulation offered a different advantage because students cannot see particle collisions. Learners could manipulate volume, observe pressure, examine a particle representation, and explain the inverse relationship at constant temperature.

After the simulation, I would include an independent transfer check to determine whether learners understood the relationship rather than merely learned how to manipulate the controls. For example, I would ask: “With the same amount of gas at constant temperature, what happens to the pressure if the volume is reduced by half, and why?” Learners would answer without using the simulation and explain the result through the frequency of particle collisions with the container walls. An accurate prediction supported by a sound particle explanation would provide stronger evidence of learning than successful manipulation of the simulation alone.

Technology has contributed to learning when students can provide accurate conceptual explanations, solve unfamiliar problems, demonstrate relevant skills, and transfer their understanding to a new situation with less avoidable difficulty. Achievement scores may complement this evidence when the assessment is aligned with the intended outcome. Confidence and learner reflections should be examined separately because they provide evidence about the learning experience, perceived barriers, and readiness, but they do not independently prove that meaningful learning has occurred (Lai & Bower, 2019).

Role of Technology and Technology Knowledge

Technology can make abstract knowledge visible, provide multiple representations, support practice and feedback, extend collaboration, document student thinking, and offer flexible ways to demonstrate understanding. The technological pedagogical content knowledge framework emphasizes that effective integration depends on relationships among content, pedagogy, and technology rather than operational knowledge alone (Mishra & Koehler, 2006).

Technology knowledge includes knowing what barrier a tool addresses, whom it may exclude, how to provide an alternative, and when costs exceed benefits. My Edmodo experience showed that initial acceptance and training were not sufficient to sustain implementation after the coordinating leadership disappeared. The implementation depended too heavily on one person to maintain the system, respond to teachers' difficulties, and encourage continued use. In a future implementation, I would name and prepare a backup coordinator, document the essential workflow, provide an accessible support guide, and schedule periodic reviews of teacher access, use, and student work. Because adoption is developmental (Straub, 2009), planning must include infrastructure, time, policy, distributed human support, and continued leadership rather than depending on a single technology advocate.

Ethical Inclusive and Accessible Practice

Ethical practice requires attention to access, privacy, academic integrity, bias, accuracy, and well-being. Learners in underserved Nigerian communities and well-resourced American schools differ in connectivity, devices, language, disability, prior knowledge, interests, and confidence. Designers should not assume equal starting opportunities. Low-bandwidth materials may be necessary where electricity or internet is unreliable; captions, transcripts, alternative text, readable contrast, keyboard access, and clear structure may remove other barriers.

Universal Design for Learning supports multiple ways to engage, access information, and demonstrate understanding (CAST, 2024). I view these as different routes toward common essential outcomes, not lower expectations. For example, learners in the Boyle's law activity could submit a written explanation, an oral explanation, or a labeled diagram. Regardless of the format, each response would be evaluated using the same essential criteria: an accurate description of the inverse relationship between pressure and volume at constant temperature, a correct prediction, and an explanation involving particle-wall collisions. Format-specific requirements would be assessed only when using that format is itself part of the learning outcome.

Artificial intelligence also requires verification, privacy protection, attention to bias, and disclosure when required. AI may support brainstorming, explanation, language, accessibility, or practice, but the student retains intellectual responsibility and the teacher retains professional judgment (UNESCO, 2023).

Technology Decision Rule

Adopt

when a tool addresses a verified need, fits the content and strategy, is reasonably accessible, and a pilot produces evidence of learning.

Adapt

when learning appears promising but instructions, cognitive load, access, culture, assessment, or accessibility create barriers.

Support further

when the tool is appropriate but confidence, infrastructure, repeated use, or implementation support remains weak.

Discontinue

when use does not improve learning, serious ethical or access problems remain, or a simpler alternative provides equal or better value.

Reflection on the Development of My Philosophy

My initial theory already emphasized active participation, intentional technology use, learner independence, collaboration, access, and the teacher's responsibility to guide and evaluate learning. The course helped me identify the theoretical foundations of those ideas and add cognitive load, UDL, framework-informed tool selection, responsible AI, adoption conditions, implementation support, and stronger distinctions among engagement, acceptance, use, and learning.

I will apply my revision rule at a clear instructional checkpoint. After supported practice, I will ask learners to independently explain or apply the concept in a new case and examine which learners still experience instructional or access barriers. I will then decide whether the evidence supports continuing the design, adapting the task, providing additional support, or changing the technology. When evidence conflicts with my assumption, I will revise the strategy or tool rather than treating the original design as fixed. My philosophy has therefore moved from a general belief in intentional technology use to a more explicit process connecting need, theory, strategy, technology, evidence, and revision.
